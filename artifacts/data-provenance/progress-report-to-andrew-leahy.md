# 1. Data: what we use, where it comes from, and why

The paper buys its data:

> "The data analyzed in this article comprises the daily total return series of
> three major equity indices: S&P 500, DAX, and Nikkei 225... These data are
> sourced from the Bloomberg Terminal. For the risk-free rates, we use the
> 3-month Treasury Bill Yield from each corresponding country, sourced from the
> Global Financial Data (GFD) database. All data spans from the start of 1970 to
> the end of 2023."

Both sources are subscription products, so every series we hold is a free
reconstruction, classified as such in the frozen research contract.

## 1.1 The binding constraint is total return, not history length

The method needs a 3000-trading-day training window plus an 8-year validation
window *before* the first tradeable day, which is why the paper's data begins in
1970 and its results begin in 1990. A series that starts late does not shorten
the sample at the front — it deletes the beginning of the reported 1990-2023
period. Required anchor is about **1970-02**.

| series | source | starts | headroom vs the anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| US equity | S&P 500 total return | 1966-01-03 | +4.1 yr |
| US cash | FRED `DTB3` | 1954-01-04 | +16.1 yr |
| DE equity | DAX performance index | 1965-01-04 | +5.1 yr |
| DE cash | three-segment ladder | 1965-01-01 | +5.1 yr |
| JP equity | Nikkei 225 total return | 1965-01-05 | +5.1 yr |
| JP cash | two-segment ladder | 1965-01-01 | +5.1 yr |

Free daily *price* history is easy; free daily *total-return* history is not.
Two of the three markets need a dividend reconstruction over part of the span,
and both sit inside the training window.

input carries a sha256, the build refuses to run if a hash moves, and nothing is
re-fetched during a run.

## 1.3 Risk-free substitutions, and where they hurt

The US needs no substitution. Germany and Japan do, because the IMF bill series
end mid-sample:

*Germany*, three segments: OECD 3-month interbank to 1975-06, IMF Germany
T-bill 1975-07 to 2007-08, ECB euro-area AAA 3-month spot thereafter. Splice
measured on the 2004-2007 overlap: −0.09pp ± 0.18. The first segment is an
interbank rate and carries a credit spread, but it only touches the warm-up.

*Japan*, two segments: IMF Japan T-bill to 2017-06, BoJ 3-month call rate
thereafter; 28-year overlap correlation 0.986.

**Japan is where this matters and I would value your view.** Japan had
essentially no Treasury bill market before 1986, so "the 3-month Treasury Bill
Yield" is whatever GFD chose to splice. Our IMF series runs about **2 percentage
points a year below** the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Japanese bill rate through
1970-1989 and 0.7pp through the 1990s, with correlation 0.972 — the shape agrees
and the level does not. JST's 1974 peak of 12.5% matches the call rate; ours
looks like an administered rate. No free 3-month Japanese *market* rate reaches
1970 (OECD's interbank series begins 2002-04), so the ambiguity cannot be
resolved, only measured — see §5.

## 2.3 Two further checks

**An independent series for the same index.** Shiller's monthly S&P 500 level
comes from an unrelated pipeline and is the monthly average of daily closes, so
our daily file must reproduce it when averaged. Across 696 months the
correlation is **0.99999328** and the median relative error **0.0005%**. Exactly
two months disagree by more than 1% (July 1974, September 2023), with
neighbouring months agreeing to four decimals and the two errors of opposite
sign — isolated bad rows in one file, not drift. The one that touches anything
we use moves the index level by 0.016% for a single month inside the training
window.

**The identity of the extreme sessions.** All twenty of the ten largest daily
gains and ten largest daily losses since 1966 fall inside October 1987, the
2008-09 crisis, the 2020 COVID crash or the 1997-98 Asian crisis; the largest
single loss is 1987-10-19 at −20.47%. A series that is correctly scaled but
wrongly dated fails this while passing every moment-based check.

# 3. Two data defects found, and fixed

Both are of the same class: invisible on 1990-2023, wrong across the training
window. Both were caught by comparing against published quantities that earlier
rounds had never used.

## 3.1 The US index was the wrong index

We had been using the CRSP value-weighted total market as a US proxy, on the
reasoning that buy-and-hold matched. It did not survive scrutiny. On 1987-10-19
the S&P 500 fell 20.47% and CRSP fell 17.41%, so every 3000-day window
containing that day fitted a high-volatility regime about **8 percentage points
below** the values the paper's Figure 2 publishes — and the model then turned
defensive across 1998-2002 where theirs stayed invested.

Switching to the real S&P 500 moved all eight US metrics toward the paper and
none away. Independent confirmation: our fixed-window persistence curve now
reproduces the paper's Table 3 to a mean error of **1.9%**, against 7.0% on the
CRSP series (§4.2).

## 3.2 The German series was missing its dividends before 1988

The DAX is a performance index, so the vendor file was treated as total return
end to end. Measured against the OECD German *share price* index — a price index
— ours runs **+3.02% a year above it over 1988-2023**, exactly as a performance
index should, and **0.15% a year below it before 1988**. The vendor spliced the
DAX Kursindex backcast onto the performance index; both are 1000.0 on
1987-12-30, so the joint leaves no trace.

Two independent confirmations. On the unrepaired series German equities return
2.67%/yr over 1970-1987 against a 6.63% cash rate — an equity risk premium of
**−3.96%/yr sustained for eighteen years** — where JST Macrohistory reports
+0.02% for the same window. And the size of the gap: 3.02%/yr measured from
OECD, against JST's 3.24%/yr German dividend yield.

Repaired by the recipe already validated for Japan: the official segment as
published, and before it the same price path plus JST annual German dividend
yields, chained onto the base value. Three gates, none of which looks at the
paper: the repaired era must carry the official era's dividend (3.24% vs 3.02%,
0.21pp apart), the equity premium must land near JST's independent figure
(−3.98% → −0.60% against +0.02%), and daily volatility must not move (0.0042pp).

**Neither repair can be fitting.** Table 4's German column covers 1990-2023,
entirely inside the untouched official segment.

## 4.1 A third finding: the drawdown definition

Table 4 does not say which wealth path its drawdown is measured on, and for a
0/1 strategy that matters by nearly six percentage points. Two published facts
settle it. Buy-and-hold's drawdowns are reproduced only with the equity leg at
total return, which rules out any excess-return path; and the caption of Figure
5 says the strategy curve is *flat* while fully invested in the risk-free asset,
so the cash leg contributes nothing. Total return when invested, nothing in
cash.

Buy-and-hold cannot test this — it is never in cash — so we recovered the
authors' own daily position paths from Figures 5 and 6. Those figures are
matplotlib vector drawings, so the shading can be read exactly; each panel's
printed annotation (bear share and regime-shift count) validates the extraction
independently, and all eight checks pass (27.8% and 96 shifts for the US HMM,
and so on for the three JM panels).

Across ten cells the mean absolute drawdown error falls from 0.0330 to **0.0072**
and mean Calmar error from 0.0262 to **0.0055**. Cross-checked out of sample on
Table 5's nine Calmar values at delays 1, 5 and 10 — of which six had never been
looked at — where mean error is 0.0235 against 0.0324 on the old basis.

# 5. The one remaining disagreement, and why it cannot be closed

Turnover fails in all three markets and nothing else does. Everything upstream
is cleared: the fixed-k curve reproduces Table 3 to 1.9%, and the authors' own
path reproduces the turnover row to 0.002. What is left is which smoothing
window the monthly cross-validation selects — and that turns out to be
unidentified at two separate levels.

**Level one: the candidate set is never published.** Section 3.4.3 describes the
selection procedure and never lists what it selects from. Sweeping eight
defensible sets, turnover spans 1.295-2.913 (US), 1.816-2.432 (DE) and
2.751-4.686 (JP) — several times the deviation under investigation in every
market. One set puts the US inside tolerance on all eight metrics; we did not
adopt it, because it helps only one market and because choosing the set that
matches is fitting to the answer.

**Level two, and this is the real result: the rule cannot choose reliably even
with the set fixed.** Splitting each 8-year validation window into halves and
comparing the argmax of each gives agreement of **17.1% (US), 15.1% (DE), 17.4%
(JP) against a chance rate of 16.7%.** Median winning margin is 0.030-0.047 of
Sharpe and about a third of months are decided by less than 0.02.

Controls, in the order we ran them:

| control | US | DE | JP |
|---|---|---|---|
| always invested vs always cash | 77.1% | 62.6% | 46.4% |
| a signal vs its own mirror image | 61.4% | 58.3% | 43.5% |
| identical paths, one handicapped by a constant 0.1 of Sharpe | 100% | 100% | 100% |

The third shows the machinery works — a consistent advantage a tenth of a Sharpe
wide is detected every time, an order of magnitude below the 0.16-0.36 spread
the real candidates show. The first two landing near chance is not the
instrument failing; it is the same phenomenon from another direction. Over
four-year windows even strategies with wildly different long-run Sharpes swap
ranks routinely — Japanese equities really did lose to cash across much of
1990-2012.

So two researchers with identical data, identical code and identical grids would
select different smoothing windows in about a third of months. Turnover is not
our defect and not a specification we could ask the authors for.

**A caveat I want to state plainly**, because it also applies to Japan's
remaining gap: our Japanese cash rate is 2pp/yr below JST's over the training
window, and refitting with JST's rate changes **0.00% of 11,507 fitted states**
while moving the reported Sharpe from 0.1771 to 0.1874 against a published 0.19.
The Japanese Sharpe therefore carries about ±0.010 from a data ambiguity the
specification cannot resolve, and the strategy itself is invariant to it. We did
not switch: JST tracks the money market and the paper asks for a bill yield.

This yields a general prior worth carrying: **errors in the tails of the
training data propagate to the fitted regimes; errors in its drift do not.** The
German dividend repair added 3.24%/yr to eighteen years of training data and
moved the German HMM Sharpe by 0.001; the CRSP substitution changed one day in
1987 and moved fitted regime volatility by eight points.

# 6. Where this stands and what is next

Complete: the data, the buy-and-hold benchmark, and the HMM baseline in all
three markets at 7/8, with the residual cell shown to be unidentifiable rather
than wrong.

Next is the jump model, which is the paper's actual contribution and the thing
we intend to extend. One prediction carries over: its penalty is chosen by the
*same* rule over the *same* window, so the same instability should appear there,
and any turnover disagreement in the JM should be read in that light before it
is treated as a modelling defect.
